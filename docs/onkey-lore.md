# Onkey: The Story So Far

Onkey arrives in a cardboard box at the home of a man who never ordered him. There is no explanation for the delivery, only a strange little creature sitting inside and a company that is already falling apart. When the man calls its support line, the employee answering is preparing to throw away the phone. The business is closing, everything is being cleared out, and nobody seems interested in taking responsibility for the unexpected arrival. Asked how to care for Onkey, the employee offers one instruction: bananas.

The man lets Onkey stay. Before long, his home is littered with banana peels, and a single week's feeding has cost him $653. Onkey fills the house with peculiar sounds and music while his owner wonders how he is supposed to afford this new life.

Then a scientist calls with an offer: $25,000 for Onkey. He speaks in a very stereotypical German accent, heavy on the z's: "zis" instead of "this", "zat" instead of "that". For a moment, the money seems like an escape from the mounting expenses. But when the owner asks what the scientist intends to do with him, the caller refuses to explain. He insists that there are plans for Onkey and suggests that surrendering him is the only way the man will get his life back. The owner decides to take his chances. He keeps Onkey and takes him out to enjoy the world beyond the house.

The scientist does not accept the refusal. He sends his enormous henchman, Man Strudel, to retrieve Onkey during their outing. Despite his intimidating appearance and knife arms, Man Strudel is friendly. He approaches the owner, asks to pet Onkey, does so carefully, and leaves without kidnapping anyone. His kindness frustrates the scientist, who regards the encounter as a failure and begins talking about upgrades. Onkey and his owner continue their day together, unaware of how closely they are being watched.

Other people have noticed Onkey too. A cowboy and a fortune-telling mirror see an opportunity to make money from him. Their plan is to steal him, put him in a cage, and use him to attract visitors who can then be sold fortunes. When they arrive outside his owner's home, they discover that the scientist has also come to steal him. The would-be kidnappers turn on one another, trading threats while hidden in the bushes.

Their confidence disappears when Onkey approaches. The mirror cannot read his mind. Neither it nor the cowboy knows whether he has discovered them, what he understands, or whether he can report their presence to his owner. A creature they had expected to capture easily has become something they cannot explain. When his owner calls him back for a banana split, the intruders retreat rather than risk finding out more.

Onkey's home has other visitors as well. Two strange inhabitants of the space beneath the floorboards invite him to join them for a party. His sleeping owner cannot come; they say he would not fit. Onkey stays where he is, and the visitors leave their invitation open. For all the attention he attracts, his life still centers on the man who feeds him and gives him a home.

Meanwhile, the scientist experiments with plush replicas. One prototype comes close enough to the original that he considers selling a legion of copies across the world. Yet the project takes a strange turn: the scientist begins responding to the plush as though it is directing him. When he questions what it stands to gain, he quickly backs down and agrees to do its bidding. Even his attempt to reproduce Onkey leaves him facing something he does not fully understand.

At Halloween, the scientist returns to the owner's house with Man Strudel, posing as trick-or-treaters. This time he plans to create a financial disaster. Man Strudel is to release M&M's whose markings, turned sideways into E's, make them evil. The candies will destroy the house, leaving the owner desperate enough to sell Onkey to pay for repairs.

But Man Strudel tries to befriend the candies. They tie him up with dental floss and threaten him with a toothpick. Onkey comes to investigate, defeats the attackers, and saves the very henchman sent to help steal him. His owner barely grasps what has happened, and the pair leave to go trick-or-treating themselves.

The scientist responds by putting Man Strudel's head into a brainbot. He considers his henchman's kindness a flaw and announces his intention to fix it. Man Strudel, still pleased with the chocolate he collected, remains friendly for the moment.

Onkey's origins remain unexplained. So does the mind the psychic could not read. Around him, people keep devising ways to capture, copy, or profit from him. Yet the bond at the center of his life is simpler: an owner who chose to keep him despite the cost, and a little creature who stays with him and helps when someone is in danger. Their ordinary pleasures—food, music, outings, and companionship—continue while the scientist prepares his next attempt.
